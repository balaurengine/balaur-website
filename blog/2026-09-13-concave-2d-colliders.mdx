A table can be one dynamic body again. `collider2d` takes a
`convex_decomposition` kind: it cuts the polygon into convex pieces, then
grows each piece through the seams it shares.

{/* truncate */}

![The same table twice, with the convex pieces the collider holds drawn over it](/img/manual/concave_pieces.webp)

Until now a concave 2D shape was a `trimesh`, which never collides with
another trimesh, or a `convex_hull`, which fills the shape in. A table lost
the gap under it.

## Why the pieces overlap

Cut a table into a top and two legs and you leave seams. A beam pushed into
one is three separate collision pairs. The top presses down, each leg presses
up, and the solver fights itself until the beam just sits there. Erin Catto
wrote this up in [Stuck Inside](https://box2d.org/posts/2020/04/stuck-inside/).

His answer is to overlap the pieces by hand. Balaur does it for you. Each
piece carries its two edges at a seam on into the neighbour and takes what
they enclose, so the legs reach up through the top and no channel is left.

`overlap` is the dial, 0.9 by default. At 0 you get the plain pieces, which
is the left half of the picture. At 1 a piece grows flush with whatever
stopped it.

## What it costs to use

Nothing to author. The collider reads the same `mesh` asset a `polygon`
draws, so the outline a player sees and the one they hit are one thing. Mass
comes from the ungrown pieces, so the table weighs what its area says rather
than counting the overlaps twice.

Imported Godot projects get it too. A solids-mode `CollisionPolygon2D` maps
to this kind, which is what Godot does with it.

Scripts can see the pieces. `geometry2d.convex_decomposition` returns them,
so a tool can draw them, move the dial, or replace one by hand.
